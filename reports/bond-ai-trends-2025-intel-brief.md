# BOND AI Trends 2025 — Intel Mining Brief

**Source date:** May 30, 2025  
**Reviewed by Wadsworth Digital:** August 12, 2026  
**Report:** 340-page PDF by Mary Meeker, Jay Simons, Daegwon Chae, and Alexander Krey  
**Primary use:** a broad, chart-heavy market map—not a live dashboard or independent validation of every underlying estimate.

## Executive read

BOND organizes the AI market around eight connected movements: faster technological change; rapid user, usage, and capital-expenditure growth; high model-development costs alongside falling inference prices; difficult monetization economics; growing competition and open-source momentum; physical-world automation; AI-native global internet growth; and rapid changes in work.[1]

The report’s most useful framing for Wadsworth Digital appears on PDF page 89 (slide 88): **“AI Agent Evolution = Chat Responses → Doing Work.”**[1] The next slide defines the shift as moving from reactive interfaces toward long-running systems that can reason, act, and complete multi-step tasks across tools. This is strategically aligned with WD’s hands-on promise: value is not another chat window, but a bounded agent that produces a usable result with permissions, guardrails, evidence, and human escalation.

## Signals worth carrying into the Intel Hub

### 1. Adoption became mass-market quickly

The report presents ChatGPT as a foundational indicator, reporting growth to 800 million weekly active users by April 2025 based on OpenAI disclosures.[1] The durable signal is not the exact count—which should be re-verified before reuse—but the compression of adoption timelines compared with earlier internet platforms.

**Intel Hub treatment:** use as historical adoption evidence labeled **“reported by OpenAI / compiled by BOND, May 2025.”** Do not present it as a current user count.

### 2. Agents represent a product-category shift

BOND’s agent section distinguishes answer generation from execution: scheduling, submitting reports, logging into tools, and orchestrating workflows.[1] This is the strongest bridge between the report and WD’s operating model.

**WD implication:** describe agents by the work they complete, the systems they can touch, their approval gates, and the evidence returned—not by anthropomorphic claims or generic “AI employee” language.

### 3. Infrastructure spending and unit economics move in opposite directions

The report pairs rising hyperscaler capital expenditure and model-training costs with steep declines in per-token inference prices. One cited Stanford HAI chart says customer inference prices fell 99.7% over two years.[1]

**Intel Hub treatment:** explain the paradox clearly: building frontier systems remains capital-intensive while consuming model output can become dramatically cheaper. Avoid implying that lower token prices automatically produce profitable AI businesses.

### 4. Revenue growth does not settle the profitability question

BOND presents estimates of OpenAI revenue and compute expense to illustrate that fast revenue growth can coexist with heavy operating costs.[1]

**WD implication:** model client-agent economics from task volume, inference/tool costs, support load, and measurable value. Do not use top-line market growth as a substitute for unit economics.

### 5. Competition is global and increasingly shaped by deployment context

The report covers proprietary leaders, open-source momentum, and the rise of Chinese models. Several comparisons depend on desktop panels, non-deduplicated users, geography restrictions, and third-party estimates.[1]

**Intel Hub treatment:** preserve those caveats on every extracted chart. Platform share is channel- and geography-dependent; it is not a universal market-share figure.

### 6. Work changes first through augmentation, workflow redesign, and skill demand

The final section combines employer memos, Census survey data, productivity research, and job-posting trends. Examples include a reported 14% productivity increase for customer-support agents using AI and rapid growth in job postings requiring AI skills.[1]

**WD implication:** lead with augmentation and operational redesign. The report includes forceful executive opinions about replacement and urgency, but those are viewpoints—not settled labor-market outcomes.

## Editorial guardrails

1. Keep **May 2025** visible anywhere the report is cited.
2. Label company disclosures and executive memos as claims or viewpoints.
3. Preserve chart notes: logarithmic axes, estimates, non-deduplicated users, geography exclusions, and sample definitions.
4. Follow important statistics to the underlying source before treating them as current.
5. Do not republish report graphics without checking BOND and upstream data-license terms; link to the report and summarize with attribution.
6. Pair the report with newer evidence when making a present-tense claim.
